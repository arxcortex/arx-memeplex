About the symbols

Root ideas carry a 3D symbol instead of a plain star. Here is what each one means and where its shape comes from.

How to read this map

This is a map of big ideas from world history: philosophies, religions and movements, from Plato, Confucius and the Buddha to internet memes. It shows where each idea came from and which ideas are alike.

What you see
  • Each glowing point is one idea. Bigger points have more "children": later ideas that grew from them.
  • Sixteen points are root ideas. Each one starts a tradition, like Plato's theory of Forms for Platonism. On the map they glow like any other point. Click one and its page shows a copper "Root idea of …" badge.
  • Lines mean "descends from". A line runs from a parent idea to a later idea that borrowed from it. Point at an idea to light up its ancestors (copper) and descendants (white).
  • Height is time. Older ideas sit low, newer ones sit high. The map ranks ideas by year rather than using an exact timeline, so crowded centuries don't squash together, and the busy last few centuries get extra height. Dates on the side line mark the eras.
  • Names appear as you zoom in. From far away you see the root ideas and the biggest ideas. Smaller ideas show their names as you get closer, and a name that would cover another one waits until there's room. Pointing at any idea shows its name.
What the floor directions mean

The floor has two fixed directions, and they always mean the same thing.

  • Left to right: material to abstract. Ideas on the right say reality is, at bottom, ideas, mind or spirit (like Plato's Forms). Ideas on the left say it is matter and physical conditions (like Epicurean atoms or Marx's economics). The middle is neutral or mixed.
  • Back to front: opposes to favors hierarchy. Ideas toward the front think some people, beings or powers rightly stand above others. Ideas toward the back oppose ranked order.

The faint labels at the edges name these four sides. Ideas with the same two scores are nudged slightly apart so they don't sit on top of each other. Ideas close together on the floor share these two stances, even if they are centuries apart; the tradition scores don't move them.

The scores, in plain words

Each idea gets a score from −5 to +5 on each of the sixteen traditions: Platonism, Aristotelianism, Stoicism, Christianity, Gnosticism, Hermeticism, Enlightenment liberalism, Marxism, Darwinism, Nietzsche, critical theory, Confucianism, Daoism, Buddhism, Vedanta and Islamic philosophy. +5 means it is that tradition or grows straight out of it. 0 means no real link. −5 means it was built to oppose it. Every score has a one-line reason. Scores are our judgment; dates and places come from cited sources.

Stance: hierarchy is a different kind of score. It asks whether the idea thinks some people, beings or powers rightly stand above others. +5 strongly favors that kind of ranked order, 0 is neutral or mixed, −5 strongly opposes it. Every idea has one, root ideas included, and it sets the front-back position.

Stance: abstract vs material asks what reality is made of. +5 means ideas, mind or spirit come first (idealist), 0 is neutral or mixed, −5 means matter comes first (materialist). It sets the left-right position.

Tone is a separate group with two scores: humor and irony. They describe how an idea is expressed, not where it comes from. They are hidden on root ideas.

How to use it
  • Click or tap an idea to open its page: a plain summary, key thinkers, parents and children, and its scores.
  • Drag to turn the map, scroll or pinch to zoom. Search finds an idea or thinker by name. Color by shows which ideas lean toward one tradition (copper) or against it (blue).
  • Compare (on an idea's page): press it, then pick a second idea to see the two side by side.
  • Share card: open an idea, or a comparison, and press Share card in its button row. It makes a tall 9:16 image for social media.
  • Filter isolates traditions: "Roots only" shows just the root ideas, and picking one or more traditions shows that root with every idea descended from it. "Branches" light up a whole family, such as All Christianity or Liberalism, and turn the camera toward it. You can combine branches and traditions.
  • Stretch pulls the map apart or squeezes it along each direction, so crowded spots open up. Use its sliders, or keys: [ and ] for abstract to material, Shift + up/down arrows to make time taller or shorter, , and . for front to back, + and − for everything, and 0 to undo it. Dots and labels keep their size.
  • Reset view (or the R key) clears filters, closes any open idea, and brings you back to the full map.
